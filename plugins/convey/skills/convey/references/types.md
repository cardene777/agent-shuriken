# Message types

Pick the one type that matches what the reader must do with the message.
Each type lists its parts in order; write the parts that have content and leave out the rest.
Every type starts with the conclusion, so a reader who stops after the first line still knows the outcome or the ask.

When the reader will open material that holds a part, such as a README, a spec, or the files themselves, leave the part to that material instead of repeating it.
The part names are for you to order the message; write each part as a sentence, never as a line that starts with the part's name and a colon.
A part is a sentence or two, or a short list, never a paragraph of background.
Numbers, dates, names, and links from the source stay exact.
When a part has nothing new, leave it out instead of writing that nothing changed.

## Progress

Where a piece of work stands, for people who follow it.

1. Conclusion: on track, late, or blocked, with the date the reader cares about
2. Done since the last update, with numbers where the source has them
3. Next, with dates
4. Blocked on what, and who can unblock it, only when something is blocked

## Decision

A choice that was made, for people it affects.

1. Conclusion: what was decided
2. Why, in one or two sentences
3. The options not taken, one line each with the reason
4. What changes for the reader, and from when
5. Who decided, and where and by when to object, when the decision is still open to change

## Request

Something you need from someone.

1. Conclusion: what you need, from whom, and by when. Mentions of the people the user named go at the start of this line
2. Why it matters, or what it unblocks
3. What they need to do it: the link, the file, or the access
4. What happens if it comes later, only when that changes their priority

## Incident

Something broken that affects people.

1. Conclusion: what is broken and the impact: who, how many, and since when
2. The current state: investigating, worked around, or fixed
3. What the reader should do now, such as a workaround, or that they need to do nothing
4. When the next update comes
5. The cause, only once it is known

## Findings

The answer to a question someone asked you to look into.

1. Conclusion: the answer to the question, in one sentence
2. The evidence: the numbers and where they come from
3. What was not checked, so the reader knows how far the answer holds
4. The recommendation, and the next step if the reader agrees

## Announcement

A change people need to know about.

1. Conclusion: what changes, and when
2. Who it affects, and what they need to do
3. Where to read more, or whom to ask

## Question

A question you need answered to move on.

1. Conclusion: the question itself, answerable in one line
2. Only the context needed to answer it
3. The options you see, with the one you would pick
4. When you need the answer
