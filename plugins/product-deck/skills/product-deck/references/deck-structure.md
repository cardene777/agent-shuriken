# The structure of a product deck

The structure and the layout discipline of a deck that explains the product itself.
It has no company profile, no prices, and no contact page.

The structure was drawn from four Japanese product decks, which set the structure and the way of annotating, and seven English ones, which set the layout and the handling of screenshots.

## What a product deck is

| | Product deck | Sales proposal |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | One to many (several prospects) | One to one (a particular customer) |
| Stage | Early: a requested or downloaded document | After a meeting |
| Content | A standard explanation | A proposal shaped by what was heard |
| Use | Read alone and considered | Used while talking |

The reader reads it with no one beside them to explain.
Make it understandable from the deck alone.

## Ten slides are the base

| # | Slide | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cover | The product's name and a one-sentence explanation |
| 2 | Overview | What the product is. Show the whole picture first |
| 3 | Problem | The reader's problem, and what happens if it is left alone |
| 4 | Contrast | The current way and the way with this product, side by side |
| 5 | Solution | How the product answers the problem |
| 6 | Features | What it can do, with screenshots |
| 7 | How to use it | The flow from starting to use it to getting results, with the screens operated |
| 8 | Scope | What it covers and what it does not |
| 9 | Why choose it | How it differs from other means |
| 10 | Frequent questions | Answers before the questions are asked |

The flow goes from the problem through the contrast to the solution, and from there to the contents and the differences.
Do not change the order.

## Put the overview second

The first thing the reader wants to know is what this is.
Do not start from the problem and hide the product until the second half.

A typical product deck builds trust with the company profile, its mission, and its track record before the main subject; this structure has none of these.
It explains the product only.

## Give the contrast a slide of its own

Five of the seven English decks had a slide of its own that set the current way beside the way with the product.
Place it between the problem and the solution.

Put a screenshot or a figure on the side of the current way too, not only text.
When only one side is a picture, it does not look like a comparison.

For a product without customers yet, this slide carries the argument: with no customer logos or user counts to show, showing the difference from today is the only means.

## Slides it does not have

| Slide | Reason |
|---|---|
| Company profile, mission, business | Not an explanation of the product |
| Track record, certifications | The same |
| Support | The same |
| Prices | The same |
| Contact | The same |

Make a separate deck when these are needed.
Do not mix them into this structure.

## When slides are added

Give each feature with a screenshot a slide of its own.
Features and How to use it grow to three to five slides with the material.

When splitting, separate the still state (what it can do) from the flow of time (how it is used).

| Slide to add | How |
|---|---|
| Features | One slide per feature, each with its screenshot |
| How to use it | One slide per step of the work |

When three or more feature slides follow each other, give them the same shape.
When each is shaped differently, the reader has to look again each time for where to look.

Keep the whole deck within 20 slides.
Split the deck when it would be longer.

## When a slide is added outside the base

Some decks need a slide that the ten do not have.
Place it between slide 5 (Solution) and slide 6 (Features).

For example, a product that follows a standard needs a slide that separates what the standard defines from what the product adds; without it the product looks like its own invention.

Write the reason for the added slide in the plan.

## Layout discipline

The level that the seven English decks shared, made measurable.

| What to decide | Discipline |
|---|---|
| The claim of a slide | One only. Write the heading as a sentence that asserts, not as a noun phrase |
| Length of the heading | Up to two lines |
| Length of the body | Up to four lines, within 200 characters per slide |
| Size of a screenshot | Its width or height is more than half the slide's |
| Colors | One main color and neutrals only. No third color |
| Section title slides | At the breaks between sections, with one line of heading only |
| Closing | One closing slide at the end |

The amount of text differs most from the Japanese decks: they put three rows of cards with about 100 characters each on a slide, and the English decks keep to a third of that or less.

## Do not

| Do not | Reason |
|---|---|
| Put two slides in a row that each line up three boxes of the same shape | It looks monotonous |
| Paste a screenshot at a quarter of the slide or less | The screen does not lead, and the product does not look alive |
| Say the same thing in the figure and the body | The reader reads the same thing twice |
| Write explanations inside a figure | The figure becomes unreadable. Put the explanation in the body |
| Show internal identifiers on a slide | Names of slide styles and frames mean nothing to the reader |

## How to show screens

Place screens along a pain or a flow.
Do not line up screens as a feature list.

| Form | Content |
|---|---|
| Pair a pain with a screen | A sentence saying what is hard in a situation, and the screenshot of that situation |
| Follow the flow of the work | A screen for each step |
| Cut out only what the claim needs | Not the whole screen, but the part where the slide's claim shows |

The plan does not fix the form for each slide.
It says what to show, and the cut and the placement are decided when the slide is built.

Do not use a screen without data.
An empty screen does not show the product working.

## Writing

| Keep | Content |
|---|---|
| Translate into the customer's words | Do not write from the provider's side. Avoid jargon |
| Match the amount of information | Too much confuses, and too little loses trust |
| Separate the details | Split into a summary and supporting material. Do not crowd a slide |
| Lean on the visual | A visual deck works better than a text deck |

## Sources

The skeleton comes from the four Japanese decks, the classification of B2B company documents by Cone Inc., and Empress's guide to making service documents.
Of their 18 slides, the 8 that do not explain the product (company profile, mission, business, track record, certifications, support, prices, contact) were removed, leaving 10.

The layout discipline, the prohibitions, and the separate contrast slide come from looking at every slide of the seven English decks.
