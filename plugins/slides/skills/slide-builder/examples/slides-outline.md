## Slide 02 / 10 ─ Where requests come in today

> meta: type=content eyebrow="Part 1 · 01"

### Takeaway

Customers write to three inboxes today: London, Paris, and Berlin.
Each desk reads only its own inbox, so nobody sees the whole picture of who is waiting.

### Explanation

Last month, the London desk received 600 requests, Paris 300, and Berlin 100.
A customer who writes to two inboxes gets two answers, or none.
When one desk is busy, the other two cannot see it and cannot help.
The team leads find out about a waiting customer only when the customer complains.

### Figure

**Purpose**: Show that one month of requests is split across three inboxes that do not see each other.

**Message**: Most requests land in London, and no desk can see the other two inboxes.

**Elements**:

- The 1,000 requests of last month as one whole
- London 600, Paris 300, Berlin 100
- A clear separation between the three inboxes

**Avoid**: A process diagram. This slide shows where requests are, not how they move.

## Slide 03 / 10 ─ What goes wrong

> meta: type=content eyebrow="Part 1 · 02"

### Takeaway

One request in ten waited more than a day for its first reply last month.
Most of these were requests that two desks each thought the other one had.

### Explanation

We looked at the 100 slowest requests of last month.
In 62 of them, the customer had written to two inboxes.
Each desk saw the other desk's name in the copy and waited.
The customer heard nothing for a day or more, and 14 of them cancelled their order.

### Figure

**Purpose**: Show how a request written to two inboxes ends up with nobody answering it.

**Message**: Two desks waiting for each other means the customer waits for both.

**Elements**:

- One customer writing to two inboxes
- Two desks, each assuming the other answers
- The day or more the customer waits

**Avoid**: Blaming one desk. The problem is that the desks cannot see each other.

## Slide 05 / 10 ─ One inbox, one owner per request

> meta: type=content eyebrow="Part 2 · 01"

### Takeaway

Every request now arrives in one inbox, and the first person who opens it becomes its owner.
Everyone can see who owns each request, so nobody waits for someone else.

### Explanation

The three old addresses still work, and they all deliver into the same inbox.
The owner's name appears next to the request as soon as it is opened.
If the owner is away, a team lead moves the request to someone else with one click.
A customer who writes twice gets one answer from one person.

### Figure

**Purpose**: Show the three old addresses feeding one inbox where each request has one named owner.

**Message**: There is one place to look, and every request in it has a name next to it.

**Elements**:

- The three old addresses
- The one shared inbox
- Each request with its owner's name

**Avoid**: A long list of requests. Three or four examples are enough.

## Slide 06 / 10 ─ How a request gets its owner

> meta: type=content eyebrow="Part 2 · 02"

### Takeaway

A request in English goes to whoever is free on any desk, and a request in French or German goes to the desk that speaks it.

### Explanation

The inbox reads the language of each request.
A request in French goes to the Paris desk first.
A request in German goes to the Berlin desk first.
If nobody on that desk opens it within one hour, everyone can take it.

### Figure

**Purpose**: Show the rule that decides which desk sees a request first.

**Message**: Language decides who looks first, and after one hour everyone can help.

**Elements**:

- A request in English, open to all three desks
- A request in French or German, open first to its desk
- The one-hour point after which everyone can take it

**Avoid**: Showing every language. Three examples are enough to explain the rule.

## Slide 07 / 10 ─ What the first month showed

> meta: type=content eyebrow="Part 2 · 03"

### Takeaway

In the first month of the trial, the first reply came after 4 hours instead of 12.
For a customer who writes in the morning, that means an answer before lunch instead of the next day.

### Explanation

We ran the shared inbox for the Paris desk first, for four weeks.
The time to the first reply fell from 12 hours to 4.
No request waited more than a day.
Cancellations after a slow reply fell from 14 to 2.

### Figure

**Purpose**: Compare the month before the trial with the first month of the trial.

**Message**: Customers now hear back on the same morning.

**Elements**:

- Time to the first reply: 12 hours before, 4 hours after
- Requests that waited more than a day: 100 before, 0 after
- Cancellations after a slow reply: 14 before, 2 after

**Avoid**: A table of numbers only. The before and after should be visible at a glance.

## Slide 09 / 10 ─ What changes for each team

> meta: type=content eyebrow="Part 3 · 01"

### Takeaway

Support answers from the shared inbox, sales forwards customer mail there instead of to a person, and product reads the weekly summary there.

### Explanation

Support: open the shared inbox instead of your desk's inbox.
Sales: forward customer mail to the shared address, not to someone you know on the desk.
Product: the inbox sends a summary every Monday with the most common requests.
Team leads: check the requests without an owner twice a day.

### Figure

**Purpose**: Show the one habit each team changes.

**Message**: Every team has exactly one thing to do differently.

**Elements**:

- Support: answer from the shared inbox
- Sales: forward customer mail to the shared address
- Product: read the Monday summary
- Team leads: check requests without an owner twice a day

**Avoid**: A calendar of the move. The dates come in a separate message.
